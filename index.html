1. Income and social support matter most

Six factors explain about 70% of the happiness gap between countries. To compare factors measured in different units, each was put on the same scale. GDP per capita leads, but having people to count on is a close second, ahead of health and freedom.

2. Some regions are happier than their economics predict

Every country was predicted using models trained without it. The gap between actual and predicted happiness shows who beats expectations. Latin America stands out, matching what researchers call the "Latin American happiness paradox."

3. A Simpson's paradox in inequality

Across all countries, higher income inequality looked linked to slightly higher happiness. That turned out to be misleading. Latin America is both very unequal and unusually happy, which masked the real pattern. Compare countries within the same region and the relationship flips.

4. Simpler won

A more flexible random forest model did not beat plain linear regression, which suggests the relationships are mostly straight-line. Adding region to the model raised accuracy further.

How it was built

  • Country-grouped validation. Models were always tested on countries they never trained on, so they couldn't memorize a country from other years.
  • Standardized coefficients to compare factors measured in different units.
  • Residual analysis to find who is happier or less happy than predicted.
  • Region controls to test whether inequality's effect changes within regions.
  • Data: World Happiness Report panel (2005 to 2024) and World Bank Gini index, pulled through the World Bank API.
  • Limitations: results show correlation, not causation. Inequality is averaged over 2000 to 2024 and missing for some countries, and happiness is self-reported.
